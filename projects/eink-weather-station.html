Overview

A battery-powered 2-layer ESP32-WROOM-32 board that pulls weather data over WiFi and paints it onto a Waveshare 4.2" four-colour e-paper panel. USB-C feeds a TP4056 single-cell LiPo charger, the cell feeds an AP2112K-3.3 LDO, and the ESP32 spends 99.3% of its life asleep.

The interesting part of this project wasn't getting it to work on paper. It was the second revision. Rev A was fully routed and looked finished, and it would not have run off a battery for a single second. Most of what follows is what that taught me.

Final state: 80 × 60 mm, 35 components, 52 nets, 49 vias, zero unrouted nets, zero DFM violations at JLCPCB's standard tier.

The Regulator That Killed Rev A

Rev A used an AMS1117-3.3, which is the default LDO in most hobby schematics and the wrong part for anything running off a lithium cell. It's an old NPN-based design with roughly 1.1 V of typical dropout and 1.3 V worst case.

A single LiPo cell sits at 4.2 V full and 3.0 V empty. Subtract the dropout and the 3.3 V rail is actually about 2.9 V on a fresh charge and closer to 2.0 V on a half-empty one, against an ESP32 minimum of 3.0 V. The board would have browned out immediately on a full cell and never started at all on a partly discharged one.

The quiescent current was independently fatal. The AMS1117 draws about 5 mA doing nothing, which flattens a 2000 mAh cell in around two weeks before the ESP32 has drawn a single milliamp.

Rev B swapped it for an AP2112K-3.3 in SOT-23-5: 250 mV typical dropout at 600 mA, 55 µA quiescent, 600 mA minimum output. Same job, one footprint change, and the idle-life arithmetic goes from weeks to months.

  • Dropout: 1.1 V typ / 1.3 V max → 250 mV typ at 600 mA
  • Quiescent current: ~5 mA → 55 µA
  • Package: SOT-223-3 → SOT-23-5 (adds an EN pin, tied to VIN for always-on)

One honest consequence worth recording: the AP2112K drops out around 3.55 V, while the battery pack's protection module doesn't cut off until roughly 2.4–3.0 V. The ESP32 browns out well before protection ever engages, so the protection circuit is a genuine last-resort net rather than part of normal operation. That also means usable capacity is about 85% of the cell, not all of it, and the runtime estimate below accounts for that.

Antenna Keep-Out and the Board Rebuild

Rev A's other problem was RF. The ESP32-WROOM module sat in the middle of the board with its antenna pointing into the interior. The footprint's keep-out region — correctly honoured by the copper pours — carved a 48 × 21 mm strip of bare FR4 straight through the middle of the board, roughly 37% of the total area, and stranded the display header on the far side of it.

Which meant six SPI signals (MOSI, SCK, EPD_CS, EPD_DC, EPD_RST, EPD_BUSY) plus two ground stitching traces all ran directly through the antenna region to reach the connector. Espressif's guidance is to keep that region clear on every layer; copper in it detunes the antenna and costs range. The board width was also being set by the keep-out rectangle rather than by anything the circuit actually needed.

Rev B moves the module to the board edge with the antenna flush against the outline, so the keep-out falls outside the board entirely, and relocates the display header. I verified the result against raw geometry rather than trusting the DRC: across the antenna window, copper is absent from the board edge down to the module's own pad row — no tracks, no vias, no foreign pads.

Power Path

USB-C (J1) → TP4056 charger (U2) → VSYS battery node → AP2112K (U3) → +3V3. Charge current is set to 500 mA by a 2.4 kΩ program resistor, which is 0.25 C into the 2000 mAh pack, and both CC pins carry their own 5.1 kΩ pulldown — sharing a single resistor across CC1 and CC2 is the classic USB-C sink mistake and it isn't present here.

The system load sits on the same node as the charger's battery pin, which normally corrupts charge termination: the TP4056 terminates by watching current fall to 1/10 of the programmed value, or 50 mA here, and it can't distinguish load from charge. I'd originally planned a discrete load-share FET to fix it. Working through the actual duty cycle showed it wasn't needed — the device is awake about 26 seconds an hour against a ~100 µA baseline, roughly 500× below the termination threshold, so termination behaves correctly.

That reasoning has a limit, and it's recorded in the board's documentation: leaving it plugged in on the bench with WiFi running continuously draws 100–150 mA, which does exceed the threshold. Charge won't terminate and the cell floats at 4.2 V. Safe, but not something to leave for days during development.

Battery sensing goes through a 1 M/1 M divider into IO34. That's ADC1, which matters: ADC2 is unusable while WiFi is active on the ESP32. A 100 nF cap at the divider tap handles the high source impedance a 1 M divider presents to the sample-and-hold, at the cost of ~250 ms of settling time after wake.

Power Budget

The design target was a display you charge a few times a year, not weekly. Estimated draw works out to about 11 mAh/day active (24 wakes of roughly 0.45 mAh each: 5 s of WiFi plus a 21 s panel refresh) plus about 2.4 mAh/day asleep, so roughly 13 mAh/day total. Against 2000 mAh with ~85% usable before LDO dropout, that's on the order of four months per charge.

These are calculated from datasheet figures, not measured — the board hasn't been built yet, and first-build sleep current is the number I most want to check. The AP2112K's 55 µA quiescent is the single largest sleep contributor, larger than the ESP32's own ~10 µA deep sleep, so a lower-Iq regulator is the obvious lever if runtime ever needs extending.

One firmware constraint falls out of the power path: WiFi has to be off before a panel refresh starts. ESP32 transmit peaks near 500 mA against the LDO's 600 mA rating, and serialising the two removes any question about headroom.

Rev A to Rev B.2

  • AMS1117-3.3 → AP2112K-3.3 — the change the project depended on
  • Added a 10 µF LDO output capacitor. Rev B.1's nearest +3V3 capacitor was 28.94 mm from the regulator's output pin, leaving it driving ~55 mm of unbypassed trace, which is a loop-stability risk. It now sits 2.34 mm from the pin.
  • Moved the ESP32 module to the board edge and rebuilt the ground pour around the relocated keep-out
  • Added a 1 M/1 M battery-sense divider into IO34
  • Added three M3 mounting holes and three fiducials, placed as a scalene triangle so the pick-and-place machine can resolve rotation as well as position
  • Deleted a dead 10 kΩ pulldown on the charger's TEMP pin, which also cleared the board's only clearance violation
  • Swapped the Omron tactile switches for generic SMD parts at a twentieth of the cost
  • 23 components / 41 vias / 48.5 × 57.5 mm → 35 components / 49 vias / 80 × 60 mm

Manufacturability

Everything sits inside JLCPCB's cheapest process tier: 0.25 mm minimum track, 0.416 mm minimum spacing, 0.3 mm minimum drill, 0.15 mm annular ring, all comfortably clear of the 0.127 mm / 0.2 mm / 0.13 mm limits. Two layers, all 27 SMD parts on the front side for single-sided assembly, only two through-hole connectors, and fiducials present. Zero violations.

The one accepted trade-off is grounding. Twenty-five track segments route on the bottom layer, cutting the board's only ground pour, and six nets change layers without a stitching via within 3 mm — worst case, the display chip-select net has 28.6% reference coverage over 50.8 mm. That's a radiated-emissions problem, not a functional one; SPI to a slow e-paper panel at a few MHz will work fine. I accepted it because this board isn't seeking certification, but it's the first thing I'd fix on a product that was.

Status & Next Steps

Design frozen August 21, 2026 and cleared for fabrication; gerbers, BOM, and pick-and-place files are generated. The build hasn't happened yet.

  • Meter the battery connector polarity before plugging in a cell — JST PH pigtails aren't consistently wired and there's no reverse-polarity protection on this board
  • Bring up on USB only, confirm 3.3 V at the display header before connecting the panel
  • Measure actual sleep current against the ~13 mAh/day estimate
  • Isolate the display module's level translator draw — it's permanently powered from the header with no way to shut it off, and if it costs tens of microamps it's worth a load switch in Rev C
