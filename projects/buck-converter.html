12 V to 5 V Buck Converter

February 2026 · Designed and routed, not yet built

  • KiCad
  • Power Electronics
  • PCB Design
  • Switching Regulator

Overview

A 78 × 16 mm two-layer board that takes 12 V in on a barrel jack and puts out 5 V on a USB-A socket at up to 3 A. It's a bench and car USB supply, and it was my first switching regulator — the point was to design a converter around a datasheet rather than buy the finished module, and to work through why each passive is the value it is.

The heart of it is an LM2596S-5, a fixed-5 V step-down regulator in TO-263 switching at 150 kHz, rated for 3 A.

Why a Switcher

A linear regulator doing 12 V to 5 V at 3 A burns the difference as heat: 7 V × 3 A is 21 W dissipated in the package, which is not a thing you can cool on a small board and is around 42% efficiency at best. A buck converter stores that energy in an inductor and hands it to the load instead, so the same conversion runs in the region of 80% efficiency and the regulator's thermal problem largely goes away.

The cost is that a switcher is a layout problem, not just a schematic problem. Every component choice below is either about keeping the inductor current continuous or about keeping the high-current loops short.

Component Selection

The design target was continuous conduction at the full 3 A load — meaning the inductor current never falls to zero during the switching cycle. Discontinuous operation isn't dangerous, but it makes the output voltage load-dependent and the ripple worse, so the passives are sized to stay out of it.

  • L1, 33 µH. Sized against the LM2596 datasheet's selection curves for a 12 V input at 3 A. The inductor sets the ripple current, and ripple is what determines whether conduction stays continuous at the low end of the load range.
  • C1, 680 µF input. A buck draws current from the input in pulses, not smoothly. The input capacitor supplies those pulses locally so they don't have to come down the supply leads, which is both a ripple and an EMI concern.
  • C2, 220 µF output. Absorbs the inductor's ripple current to give a flat 5 V rail, and sets transient response when the load steps.
  • D1, SS54 Schottky. 40 V, 5 A. This is the catch diode that carries inductor current during the switch-off half of the cycle, so it conducts most of the time at this duty ratio. Schottky rather than silicon for the lower forward drop and fast recovery; the 40 V and 5 A ratings leave headroom above the 12 V input and 3 A load for switching transients.

Layout

The input, switch node, and output traces are all widened to 0.8 mm to carry 3 A without meaningful drop or heating. The switch node is the one that matters most for noise: it's the junction of the regulator's output pin, the inductor, and the catch diode, and it swings the full input voltage at 150 kHz. Kept short, it's a trace; let it sprawl and it's an antenna.

The 78 × 16 mm outline is deliberately long and narrow so the barrel jack and the USB-A socket sit at opposite ends, giving a clean straight-through power path with the switching section in the middle.

Status

The schematic and layout are complete in KiCad. The board hasn't been fabricated or brought up, so every number on this page is a design figure rather than a measurement. When it's built, the things worth putting on a scope are the switch node waveform, output ripple at full load, and efficiency across the load range against the ~80% the datasheet implies.
